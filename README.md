# PROJECT VULCAN

**Vehicle for Unmanned Laying, Conversion, & Autonomous Navigation**

A collaborative student engineering-design challenge: imagine a repurposed
locomotive that scouts terrain, reclaims usable rail, and positions track ahead
of itself. Students explain how its subsystems would work together and investigate
the engineering trade-offs.

**Status: curriculum starter, not yet a classroom-ready lesson package.**
This is not a full-scale construction project. The core outcome is a reasoned
design proposal supported by drawings, calculations, simulations, or optional
tabletop models—not a working autonomous machine.

## Age groups

| Grades | Learning focus | Appropriate evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4–6: Explore | Vehicle functions, simple machines, teamwork | Annotated drawings, cardboard mechanisms, hand-positioned model track using teacher-prepared materials |
| 7–8: Model | Gears, linkages, subsystem interfaces, basic sensing | Subsystem models and optional supervised low-voltage sensor demonstrations |
| 9–12: Core project | Systems engineering, CAD, energy budgets, feedback logic, feasibility and safety | Integrated design proposal, calculations, simulation or optional tabletop prototype, and design review |

Use age-appropriate tools and adapt activities to student experience, accessibility
needs, available supervision, and school requirements. The four-week outline below
is for grades 9–12; younger groups use selected, simplified activities.

## Engineering teams

1. **Chassis and drivetrain:** Explore how rail-bound movement could transition
   to another support system. Compare wheels, outriggers, crawlers, and lifting
   linkages, including traction, stability, mass, and pinch-point risks.
2. **Tooling and recycling:** Diagram inspection, fastener removal, rail lifting,
   ballast leveling, tie placement, and rail positioning. Distinguish reusable
   rail from rejected scrap; reshaping alone does not prove worn rail is safe.
   Represent cutting, welding, and heating through simulation.
3. **Scouting and sensors:** Explain how terrain information affects speed,
   stopping, and track placement. Match sensors to measurements: ultrasonic
   distance readings alone do not establish slope or ground compaction.
4. **Energy and pull-carts:** Estimate propulsion, tooling, and sensing demands.
   Compare peak power (watts) with total energy (watt-hours), and account for
   cart loads, gradients, losses, and runtime.

Each team identifies its inputs, outputs, assumptions, and dependencies on other
teams. Full-size industrial ideas may remain in the concept without becoming
student fabrication activities.

## Feasibility and safety boundaries

- Treat locomotive transformation and continuous rail processing as design
  hypotheses to evaluate, not established capabilities.
- A 2% grade does not universally double motor power. State mass, resistance,
  speed, and efficiency assumptions before comparing energy demands.
- A proposed 5% scout stopping threshold is a simulation setting, not a
  validated safe operating limit.
- For a 1:20 geometric model, standard-gauge rail spacing is approximately
  71.8 mm between the rails. Model behavior does not directly validate full-size
  strength, traction, or energy performance.
- No real railway work, locomotive disassembly, hot processing, welding,
  powered rail cutting, pressure systems, or student-built battery packs.
- Teachers screen donor materials and prepare parts. Exclude damaged batteries,
  mains power supplies, microwave ovens, CRT displays, and unidentified capacitors.
  Salvaged equipment may contain sharp edges, hot parts, or stored energy.
- Optional powered models require teacher-approved, enclosed, current-limited
  low-voltage power with appropriate overcurrent protection. Guard pinch points,
  secure loads, and provide a readily accessible physical motor-power cutoff.
- Define and test model mass, speed, load, slope, and gap limits before student
  operation. Use a bounded tabletop area with a catch surface beneath any gap;
  disconnect power before clearing jams.
- Powered autonomy must stop locally on stale or missing telemetry, invalid
  sensor readings, or jams. Wireless commands alone are not a safety interlock.

The teacher must review the selected activities and local safety requirements
before classroom use. A paper/CAD/simulation-only route is a complete option.

## Four-week learning outline

| Week | Focus | Student deliverable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Form teams, select scope, compare concepts, define interfaces | Annotated concept drawing, subsystem map, and initial assumptions |
| 2 | Research materials and mechanisms; inventory teacher-approved model parts only if needed | Materials rationale, mechanism study, and draft energy budget |
| 3 | Develop scout feedback and integrate team designs | Control-flow diagram or simulation, interface review, and fault-response cases |
| 4 | Evaluate the closed-loop challenge and revise | Demonstration or simulation evidence, feasibility/safety review, and final presentation |

The baseline challenge is **sense → stop → position a rail module → advance**.
It may be shown through a storyboard or simulation. Optional models use reusable
plastic or wooden track and a short, supported test area. Automatic placement and
transformation are extensions, not completion requirements.

## Guided completion workflow

Work through these checkpoints in order. Record decisions below, then use the
next unfinished checkpoint to guide the next discussion. This is a curriculum
development workflow, not an automated software build.

### 1. Confirm the classroom scope — start here

- [ ] Choose the first grade band and students' prior experience.
- [ ] Choose the route: drawings/CAD, simulation, optional tabletop model, or a mix.
- [ ] Confirm session count and length, team size, budget, and available tools.
- [ ] Identify accessibility needs and an equivalent non-fabrication route.

**Checkpoint:** Scope is approved before selecting activities or buying parts.

### 2. Define learning and assessment

- [ ] Select measurable objectives for mechanisms, sensing, energy, and teamwork.
- [ ] Define required student evidence and any curriculum standards alignment.
- [ ] Create a rubric for reasoning, evidence, interfaces, safety, and iteration.
- [ ] Set age-appropriate success criteria; do not require a working robot.

**Checkpoint:** Every objective has an observable deliverable and an assessment.

### 3. Prepare the teaching package

- [ ] Expand each week into sessions with teacher preparation and student tasks.
- [ ] Prepare subsystem briefs, interface questions, and an assumptions log.
- [ ] Prepare an energy-budget worksheet that separates power from energy.
- [ ] Provide sensor limitations, fault scenarios, and design-review prompts.
- [ ] Finalize the approved materials list and activity-specific safety review.

**Checkpoint:** A teacher can deliver each session without guessing prerequisites
or safety boundaries.

### 4. Pilot and refine

- [ ] Walk through the paper/simulation route before classroom delivery.
- [ ] If models are included, verify guards, power cutoff, load stability, and
      safe stops for sensor faults, communication loss, and jams.
- [ ] Run a small supervised pilot and record observations against the objectives.
- [ ] Adjust activities, pacing, accessibility, and assessment using that evidence.

**Checkpoint:** Resolve safety issues and unclear instructions before wider use.

### 5. Complete the first curriculum release

- [ ] Finalize teacher guidance, student briefs, worksheets, and rubric.
- [ ] Identify researched claims, cite sources, and label unresolved assumptions.
- [ ] Review the final package for age suitability, accessibility, and safety.
- [ ] Record what was piloted, known limitations, and optional future extensions.

**Completion standard:** A reviewed teaching package with a viable non-build
route and evidence-based assessment—not a certified industrial design.
